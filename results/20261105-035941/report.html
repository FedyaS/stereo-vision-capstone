01

Summary

23 stereo matching methods were run on 15 rectified pairs with ground truth, scored on the nonocc region and profiled for time and memory on one machine. The most accurate was learned_raft_stereo_r4 at 10.5% of pixels off by more than 2 px; the fastest was opencv_bm at 0.026 s per pair (36.4%). The run's main result is that the cost function dominates: with everything else held fixed, swapping the cost function moves the error by 18.7 points against 12.7 for the aggregation strategy. On the pairs that differ only in exposure or lighting, structure-based costs lose +1.3 points where intensity-based costs lose +25.6. Every error figure appears twice — once counting unanswered pixels as errors, once over answered pixels only — because the two orderings are different and a method can be made to look good by answering less.
02

What the numbers mean

Each statement below is derived from this run's data, not written in advance. The supporting measurement is given under each one.

●

learned_raft_stereo_r4 is the most accurate method here

headline

It leaves 10.5% of evaluated pixels more than 2 px from the truth, averaged over 15 scenes, and takes 4.43 s per pair. Read that number as an upper bound on quality for this field, not as a good result in absolute terms — one pixel in 10 is still wrong.

mean over the nonocc region, 23 algorithms compared

●

A from-scratch matcher is competitive with the libraries

headline

scratch_census_sgm4 — a few hundred lines of NumPy — scores 12.4%, and it lands 1.9 points behind learned_raft_stereo_r4, while beating the best opencv entry. Classical stereo is not a black box: the accuracy is in the cost function and the aggregation, both of which fit on a page.

learned: 10.5%, torch: 12.3%, scratch: 12.4%, opencv: 22.7%

●

The cost function matters more than the other half of the pipeline

headline

Holding the pipeline fixed and changing only the cost function moves bad 2px by 18.7 points (ssd → census: 36.0% → 17.3%). Changing only the aggregation moves it by 12.7 (block matching → semi-global block matching: 36.4% → 23.7%). How you compare two patches is the decision that counts; smoothing a bad cost volume cannot recover what the comparison threw away.

the same family, aggregation and post-processing (scratch / wta); the same family, cost function and post-processing (opencv)

●

Only structure-based costs survive an exposure change

headline

The dataset ships pairs that differ in exposure or lighting but not in geometry, so the extra error is caused by the photometric change and nothing else. Costs that compare structure (census, ZNCC) gain +1.3 points on average; costs that compare raw intensity (SAD, SSD) gain +25.6. The worst case is scratch_sad_wta_lrcheck at +53.5. This is the single clearest mechanism in the whole run: census discards brightness and keeps only the ordering of neighbouring pixels, so a gain change is invisible to it.

8 invariant vs 5 intensity-based methods

⚠

Sparse methods look far better than they are

caution

opencv_bm answers only 71% of pixels. Score it only where it answered and it is 10.5% wrong, ranking #2; count the pixels it skipped as errors — which is what a downstream consumer of a depth map experiences — and it is 36.4% wrong, ranking #20. This is why every error figure in this report is given twice. A method that declines to guess is not thereby accurate.

density 71%, gap 25.9 points between the two framings

⚠

Wall time hides what a method actually costs

caution

torch_sad_wta and scratch_sad_wta compute the same answer (35.8% and 35.8%) in a comparable wall time (2.19 s and 1.79 s), but the first burns 30 CPU-seconds to the second's 1.8 — a 16× difference in energy for the same result. On a laptop sharing cores with anything else, or on a robot with a power budget, wall time is the wrong column.

cpu_s / wall_s is the 'cores busy' column

⚠

learned_raft_stereo_r1 did not converge — do not read it as a result

caution

Its average error is 25.2 px against a field median of 2.4 px, at 61.2% bad pixels. An error that size on a dense output is not a hard scene, it is a method running outside the conditions it needs — too few refinement iterations, or an input normalisation that does not match what the weights expect. It is kept in the table because removing rows that disappoint you is how benchmarks start lying.

field median EPE 2.4 px

○

The accuracy you can buy with time, and where it stops

detail

7 of 23 methods are not beaten on both axes at once. Moving along that frontier from opencv_bm to learned_raft_stereo_r4 costs 174× the time and buys 25.9 points of accuracy. Everything off the frontier is strictly dominated — slower and less accurate than something else in the table — and needs a reason other than accuracy or speed to be chosen.

○

Averages over scenes hide where the methods differ

detail

PianoL is the hardest scene in the run (mean 44.8%, best 17.4% by learned_raft_stereo_r4) and Motorcycle the easiest (14.5%). But the scene worth looking at is Playtable, where the field spans 78 points from 6.6% to 84.9%. A method's mean can look respectable while it collapses on one scene type, which is the failure mode that matters if you only ever deploy on that type.

03

How to read the metrics

A stereo result cannot be summarised by one number, because "wrong" has at least three meanings: how often, how far, and how much of the image was answered at all.

Accuracy — was it right?

bad2_all bad 2px %lower is better
The headline number. For every pixel in the scoring region, is the estimate within 2 px of the truth? A pixel the method declined to estimate counts as wrong, which is the only framing that compares a dense method with a sparse one honestly. 2 px is the Middlebury convention; it is loose enough to ignore sub-pixel noise and tight enough that a surface at the wrong depth fails it.
bad2_valid bad 2px, answered %lower is better
The same test restricted to pixels the method answered. This measures the quality of the answers it chose to give, which is the right question when a later stage will fill the holes anyway — and the wrong one if nothing will.
bad1_all bad 1px %lower is better
Tightening the threshold separates methods that get the surface roughly right from methods that get it precisely right. A method with good bad 2px and poor bad 1px is finding the correct surface but quantising it — usually sub-pixel refinement turned off.
bad0p5_all bad 0.5px %lower is better
Almost entirely a test of sub-pixel interpolation. Without it, disparities are integers and roughly half of all pixels fail this by construction.
epe_valid EPE pxlower is better
End-point error: the average of |estimate − truth| in pixels. Where bad N asks how often a method is wrong, EPE asks how far. A mean is dragged by outliers, so a method with a low bad-pixel rate and a high EPE is mostly right with a few catastrophically wrong regions — typically a repeated texture matched to the wrong period.
rmse_valid RMSE pxlower is better
Squaring before averaging weights a single 20 px blunder like a hundred 2 px wobbles. Compare it against EPE: a large ratio means the error is concentrated in a few places rather than spread thin.
a95 A95 pxlower is better
A quantile, so no single blunder can move it. Read it as the error you should expect in the worst 1 pixel in 20 — a more useful promise than a mean for anything that has to be safe rather than accurate on average.
bias_valid bias pxcloser to zero is better
The average of (estimate − truth), with its sign kept. Zero means the errors cancel; a consistent offset points at a calibration or convention problem — an off-by-one in the disparity indexing, or a sub-pixel refinement biased toward the cost minimum's left side — rather than at matching quality.
density density %higher is better
The denominator behind every '…, answered' column. A method that answers 70% of pixels is being graded on a self-selected 70%. Always read it next to accuracy: a consistency check that raises confidence by discarding ambiguous pixels improves every 'answered' metric and worsens every honest one.

Cost — what did it take?

wall_s wall slower is better
Clock time for one pair, median over the configured repeats. It is what latency means, and it is also the noisiest number in the report: a laptop that has warmed up and dropped its clocks drifts by more than the differences being measured. Compare within a run, never across runs.
cpu_s CPU slower is better
Total processor time, summed over every core. This is the figure proportional to energy and to what you are renting. Two methods with the same wall time and a 20× gap in CPU time are not equally cheap.
parallelism cores busy ×neither good nor bad
1.0 means single-threaded; 16 means sixteen cores were busy throughout. A single-threaded method's wall time understates how much cheaper it is than a method of the same speed spreading across the machine — and overstates how it will behave once something else wants those cores.
mdps Mdisp/shigher is better
Throughput normalised by both image size and search range, so it compares a small image with a wide disparity range against a large image with a narrow one. The honest way to compare speed across scenes of different sizes.
mpix_per_s Mpix/shigher is better
Throughput normalised by image size only. Easier to reason about than Mdisp/s, but it flatters a method run on a scene with a narrow disparity range.
rss_delta_mb RSS growth MBlower is better
The memory attributable to this method: resident set size at the end minus at the start, sampled on a background thread. An under-estimate for a method reusing memory the allocator already holds, so compare within a family first.
peak_rss_mb process peak MBneither good nor bad
Carries everything earlier cells in the same process allocated, so it is an upper bound on the process, not a measurement of the algorithm. Re-run one method alone if you need a clean number.

The families

  • learned — a pretrained deep network, run through ONNX Runtime (marker: diamond)
  • opencv — OpenCV's own matchers, C++ and SIMD-optimised (marker: circle)
  • scratch — written here in NumPy, to be read rather than to be fast (marker: square)
  • torch — the same maths as scratch, re-expressed as tensor operations so it can move to a GPU (marker: triangle)

What this does not show

The limits of the experiment, stated so a number from it is not quoted further than it can carry.

Timing is only comparable within this run

Every cell here was timed as the median of 1 run(s) on one machine in one session. A laptop that has warmed up and dropped its clocks drifts by more than the differences between neighbouring rows, and two runs of this benchmark hours apart have been measured to differ by over 2× on identical code. Ratios inside this table are meaningful; absolute seconds quoted against another run are not. With a single repeat there is no variance estimate at all — re-run with --repeats 5 before quoting any individual number.

Memory is an under-estimate

RSS growth is sampled on a background thread, so a short-lived peak between samples is missed, and a method reusing memory the allocator already holds appears to allocate nothing. process peak carries everything earlier cells allocated and is not this method's footprint. Compare within a family first.

Every pair here is already rectified

Matching only ever searches along a row. Rectification — and the calibration behind it — is assumed perfect and is not measured, yet it is where a real system's error usually comes from. A method that wins here can still lose on a rig whose epipolar lines are a pixel off.

Indoor scenes, one dataset

These are controlled Middlebury captures: static, textured, evenly lit, indoors. Nothing here predicts behaviour on a wet road at night, on a specular surface, or on anything moving. The ordering of methods is reasonably stable across datasets; the absolute numbers are not.

Defaults were tuned, and tuning can overfit

Per-algorithm defaults in this project came from a grid search restricted to a tune split, with the remaining scenes held out. This run scores both splits together, which is the honest headline number but mixes scenes the parameters saw with scenes they did not. The scene table marks which is which.

A failed method is still a method

Rows that did not converge are kept in the table and flagged. Dropping them would raise every average and make the field look tighter than it is.
